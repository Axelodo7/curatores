Part 2 · Introduction

Declensions

Declensions work a little like the ’s and s of English, which show possession (the girl’s book) and plurals (two girls). A Latin ending does more: it shows a noun’s case and number, and usually its gender too.

  • Case

    the job a noun does

    You met the cases in Capitulum I: subject, object, possession and the rest.

  • Gender

    masculine, feminine or neuter

    Every noun has one, and it goes with the endings: most nouns in -a are feminine, most in -us masculine, most in -um neuter.

  • Number

    singular or plural

    Whether there is one of something or more than one.

First declension

Mostly feminine nouns ending in -a, like puella and pecunia. Here is mensa, table.

Four forms end in -ae, so the rest of the sentence tells you which case is meant.

Second declension, masculine

Mostly masculine nouns ending in -us, like filius, son.

Nouns in -ius shorten the vocative to -ī, so a father calls his son filī.

Neuter nouns ending in -um, like templum, temple.

Sample sentences

Read each sentence, then open it to check your translation. The endings you just met are marked in gold.

  1. Filia mensam videt.

    The daughter sees the table.

    Filia is the subject, so it is nominative. Mensam is the object, so it is accusative. Both are first declension.

  2. Pater filiō pecuniam dat.

    The father gives his son money.

    Filiō is dative, second declension: the son receives. Pecuniam is accusative, first declension: the thing given.

  3. Quis pecuniam filiī videt?

    Who sees the son’s money?

    Filiī is genitive, second declension: the money belongs to the son.

  4. Puer improbus mensam verberat.

    The wicked boy beats the table.

    Improbus describes puer, a masculine noun, so it takes the masculine ending -us.

  5. Cur filia parva plorat?

    Why is the little daughter crying?

    Parva describes filia, a feminine noun, so it takes the feminine ending -a.

  6. Filius interrogat, sed filia respondet.

    The son asks, but the daughter answers.

    Filius and filia: one root, two genders, two declensions.

  7. Num puer magnus plorat?

    Surely the big boy isn’t crying?

    Num asks a question that expects the answer no.

Part 3

Etymology

Find out how Latin is still used today. Open each field to see its Latin.

Rome built in stone, and its builders’ words are still on the job. Each term is shown with the Latin it comes from.

General terms

  • Atrium

    ātrium, the central hall of a Roman house

    A large central hall or open courtyard in a building.

  • Aqueduct

    aqua, water, and ductus, leading

    A structure that carries water from one place to another.

  • Fenestration

    fenestra, window

    The arrangement of windows in a building.

  • Exterior

    exterior, outer

    The outside of a building or structure.

  • Interior

    interior, inner

    The inside of a building or structure.

Architectural designs

  • Aedicule

    aedicula, small shrine, from aedes, temple

    A small house or shrine, or a temple-shaped frame around a door or window.

  • Apse

    apsis, arch

    A rounded recess in the wall of a building, often at the end of a church.

Two roots from Capitulum I, manus and oculus, at work in a doctor’s office.

Manus · hand

  • Manual manipulation

    manus, hand

    A treatment in which a doctor’s hands apply pressure to part of the body.

  • Manual lymph drainage

    manus, hand

    A massage technique meant to reduce swelling in the body.

  • Manifestation

    manifestus, plain to see, probably from manus

    How a disease shows itself in a patient.

Oculus · eye

  • Oculography

    oculus, eye

    A technique for observing and recording eye movement.

  • Oculomotor muscles

    oculus, eye, and motor, mover

    The muscles that move the eye.

  • Oculus dexter, sinister, uterque

    right, left and both eyes

    Eye prescriptions still shorten them to OD, OS and OU.

Courts still use whole Latin phrases. The gold line under each is its direct translation. You met quid as “what?” in this chapter’s vocabulary; in quid pro quo it means “something.”

  • Habeas corpus

    “you shall have the body”

    The procedure that lets a person challenge their detention or imprisonment.

  • Pro bono publico

    “for the public good”

    Legal work done voluntarily, without payment, for someone who cannot afford it.

  • Mens rea

    “guilty mind”

    The intent a person had when they committed a crime.

  • Actus reus

    “guilty act”

    The physical acts that make an action a crime.

  • Amicus curiae

    “friend of the court”

    Someone who is not part of a lawsuit but offers information to help the court decide it.

  • Bona fide

    “good faith”

    Something real and not fake.

  • Caveat emptor

    “let the buyer beware”

    The principle that it is the buyer’s responsibility to check for any defects before buying.

  • Quid pro quo

    “this for that”

    A trade or exchange between two parties.

  • Ad hominem

    “to the man”

    Attacking a person’s character instead of their argument in a debate.

  • Pro forma

    “for form”

    Done as a formality, following standard procedure, rather than reflecting the actual final outcome.

Fossil hunters name their finds in Latin, often mixed with Greek. Each name is shown with its meaning and the Latin in it.

  • Tyrannosaurus rex

    “king tyrant lizard”

    rex: king

  • Velociraptor

    “swift thief”

    velox: swift, quick · raptor: thief, robber

  • Carnotaurus

    “meat-eating bull”

    caro, carnis: flesh · taurus: bull

  • Fossilization

    from fossilis, “dug up”

    The slow process that turns buried remains into fossils.

  • Extinct

    from exstinguere, “to put out”

    No longer living anywhere, like a fire that has gone out.
